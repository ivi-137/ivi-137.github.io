Ask a language model to read this notebook and write the next entry. It will get the topic right and the voice roughly right. Then, often somewhere around the fourth paragraph, a formula will appear between single dollar signs, although every formula here sits between two. A heading will arrive in Title Case. "Behaviour" will lose its u. And the post will stop one section early, without the sources that close this one. None of this is hard. Each is a promise the earlier posts made, and each gets broken anyway.

I'll call the property that breaks *coherence*: a text is coherent when it keeps the obligations its context created, from the first token to the last. This post makes one argument about it. A transformer has memory but no state. It keeps every token it has seen, but it carries nothing forward from one step to the next except those tokens, so every obligation has to be re-derived, at every step, by attention. And attention pays for everything out of a single budget that sums to 1 and is shared with a context that grows by one token per step. Three failures follow, each provable: the obligation's share of attention *dilutes*, small per-step slips *compound* over a long text, and the decision to stop is made *blind* to what is still owed. The repair is to give obligations what the transformer lacks: a small state of their own, read through a budget of its own, connected to the decision to stop. I call it the *Ledger*. For one important class of obligations, those that pick one variant among interchangeable ones, group theory goes further: the state collapses to a single symmetry, read once, and those obligations can no longer fail at any length.

The contributions, each with its evidence:

1. **A diagnosis.** Dilution (Lemma 1, and Corollary 1: holding an instruction's share needs a score edge that grows like $$\log n$$), compounding (Proposition 2: the chance of keeping every invariant falls exponentially with length) and blind stopping (Proposition 3: a closed form for ending with a debt unpaid). In a controlled experiment, the product law of Proposition 2 predicts how often posts keep all their invariants to within half a point.
2. **The Ledger.** Typed slots filled once from the prompt, a state per slot updated by every written token, a read with its own softmax, and a gate on the end-of-text token. It computes exactly the original function when inserted. In the experiment it lifts coherent posts from 80.5% to 90.0% ($$p = 5.5 \times 10^{-8}$$) and the right length from 38.5% to 98.5%, and the same slots inside the ordinary softmax give nothing (74.0%).
3. **Gauge fixing.** When conventions are orbits of a group acting on the vocabulary, reading the author's frame once and writing in a canonical frame makes them hold at every length with probability at least $$1 - \sum_i e^{-2 m_i (1/2 - \eta_i)^2}$$ (Theorem 1). In the experiment, not one of 4,526 opportunities to break them was taken, and the model could stop attending to its examples: 64% fewer keys per written token.
4. **Where it belongs.** Each part answers one failure, and each failure happens in one place in the network; placement follows, and a screen of six placements agrees with one correction.

The experiment is a toy trained on four CPU cores, and I say so wherever it matters. The mathematics carries the argument; the toy checks that the mechanisms do what the mathematics says.

## The machine

### Tokens and positions

Ashish Vaswani and seven co-authors introduced the Transformer in 2017, as a translation model. Text is cut into tokens, and each token becomes a vector of $$d_{\text{model}} = 512$$ numbers. There is no recurrence and no convolution, so nothing in the model knows which token came first. Order has to be added. Each position $$p$$ gets a fixed vector, added to the token's own:

$$
\mathrm{PE}(p, 2i) = \sin\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}, \qquad \mathrm{PE}(p, 2i+1) = \cos\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}.
$$

Each pair of dimensions is a clock running at its own speed, with wavelengths from $$2\pi$$ to $$10000 \cdot 2\pi$$ positions. The authors chose clocks because, for any fixed offset $$k$$, moving from $$\mathrm{PE}(p)$$ to $$\mathrm{PE}(p+k)$$ is a rotation in every pair, a linear map. The dot product of two positions then depends only on how far apart they are:

$$
\mathrm{PE}(p) \cdot \mathrm{PE}(p+k) = \sum_{i} \cos(\omega_i k), \qquad \omega_i = 10000^{-2i/d_{\text{model}}}.
$$

<div data-posenc data-caption="Sinusoidal positions: 96 positions by 128 dimensions, green positive and blue negative. Below, one position's similarity with all the others. Move the slider and the curve only shifts."></div>

Most current models use a descendant, rotary embeddings, which apply the same rotations to queries and keys instead of adding them to the input. The toy model later in this post does too.

### Attention

Each token emits three vectors, all linear maps of its own: a query $$q$$, a key $$k$$ and a value $$v$$. With the queries, keys and values of all tokens stacked into matrices,

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V.
$$

Read it one row at a time. One token's query is compared with every key by a dot product. The scores $$s_j$$ become weights through the softmax, $$a_j = e^{s_j} / \sum_i e^{s_i}$$, and the output is the weighted average of the values. Every weight is positive, and the weights in a row add up to 1. Keep that last fact in view. Most of this post follows from it.

Why divide by $$\sqrt{d_k}$$? If the components of $$q$$ and $$k$$ are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product has variance $$d_k$$ (the paper's footnote 4). Without the division, scores grow with the width of the head, the softmax saturates towards a single winner, and its gradients vanish. Try it:

<div data-attention data-caption="Scaled dot-product attention over one sentence. Click a word to make it the query. Widen d_k with the scaling switched off and the weights collapse onto one word."></div>

A model that writes left to right must not look at tokens it has not written yet, so the scores of later positions are set to $$-\infty$$ before the softmax. That is the causal mask.

### Heads, layers and training

Instead of one attention, the model runs $$h = 8$$ of them in parallel, each on its own 64-dimensional projections, and concatenates the results:

$$
\mathrm{MultiHead}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{Concat}(\mathrm{head}_1, \dots, \mathrm{head}_h)\, W^O, \qquad \mathrm{head}_i = \mathrm{Attention}(QW_i^Q, KW_i^K, VW_i^V).
$$

Each head is free to look for something different: the previous word, the subject of a verb, a matching bracket. After attention, every position passes on its own through a two-layer network, $$\mathrm{FFN}(x) = \max(0,\, xW_1 + b_1)\,W_2 + b_2$$, with an inner width of 2048. Each of these sub-layers is wrapped as $$\mathrm{LayerNorm}(x + \mathrm{Sublayer}(x))$$: a residual connection, so information can pass a layer untouched, and a normalisation. Six such layers make the encoder. The decoder has six more, with a third sub-layer that attends to the encoder's output.

<TransformerDiagram />

Training used Adam with $$\beta_1 = 0.9$$, $$\beta_2 = 0.98$$ and a learning rate that rises for 4,000 steps and then decays with the inverse square root of the step,

$$
\mathrm{lr} = d_{\text{model}}^{-0.5} \cdot \min\!\left(\text{step}^{-0.5},\ \text{step} \cdot 4000^{-1.5}\right),
$$

with dropout and label smoothing both at 0.1. The big model reached 28.4 BLEU on English to German and 41.8 on English to French, after 3.5 days on eight P100 GPUs.

For this post, the most important part of the paper is its Table 1, which compares layer types:

| layer | cost per layer | sequential steps | longest path between two tokens |
|---|---|---|---|
| self-attention | $$O(n^2 d)$$ | $$O(1)$$ | $$O(1)$$ |
| recurrent | $$O(n d^2)$$ | $$O(n)$$ | $$O(n)$$ |
| convolutional | $$O(k n d^2)$$ | $$O(1)$$ | $$O(\log_k n)$$ |

In a recurrent network, a word at the start of a document has to survive $$n$$ updates before it can influence the end. In a transformer the path is one step long: any token reads any other directly. That is a large part of why transformers replaced recurrent networks, and it seems to make forgetting impossible. It doesn't. A path of length one still has a width, and the width is paid for out of a budget that adds up to 1.

Today's language models keep only the decoder. They are trained to predict the next token,

$$
p(y \mid c) = \prod_{t=1}^{L} p(y_t \mid c,\, y_{<t}),
$$

and they write by sampling one token at a time and feeding it back in. Every rule the text has to follow must be recovered, at every step, by attention over a context that grows by one token each time.

### Two facts the rest depends on

**Fact 1: one budget.** Every row of attention is a probability distribution over all the tokens visible to it. Whatever a head gives to one token it takes from the others, and the number of others grows by one with every token written.

**Fact 2: memory without state.** Between two steps, a decoder carries nothing forward except the tokens themselves, together with their keys and values, which are fixed views of those tokens. There is no small running summary, of the kind a finite automaton keeps, that the next step inherits. So anything the text has committed to, a font, a count, a debt of sources, must be found again at every step by attention over the whole context. The transformer's great strength, that any token can read any other directly, is also the reason it has to.

Everything below is a consequence of these two facts, and the repair adds exactly what the second one says is missing.

## How often models lose the thread

No single benchmark measures coherence as I have defined it. The nearest measurements come from three directions: instructions a program can check, long inputs, and imitation of a style.

The rows sort into the four lines of the table at the top. Failures that grow with the length of the context (RULER, NoLiMa, lost in the middle, persona drift, Levy et al.) are what dilution predicts. Failures that grow with the number of rules and turns (IFEval, Multi-IF, IFScale, Laban et al.) are what compounding predicts: every extra rule and every extra step is another chance to slip. Failures at the end, missing citations above all, are what blind stopping predicts. And the hardest case, imitation from examples, combines all three with a fourth difficulty: nobody states the rules, so they must first be read off the examples.

Two more observations shape the repair. Laban et al. separate *aptitude*, which barely moved, from *reliability*, which collapsed: the models could do the task, they just did not do it every time. That points at the mechanism, not the knowledge. And newer models do better on every row, but each study found failures that grow with length, turns or the number of rules, across models, labs and years. A pattern that stable points to the architecture.

## What coherence means

### Obligations

In 1957 Noam Chomsky wrote a sentence to make a point about grammar: "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously." Every word is in its place, and the sentence means nothing. Being well formed and making sense are different properties, and coherence, in the sense I need, is a third one, closer to bookkeeping. A context makes promises about the text that follows it. The earlier posts in this notebook promise a way of writing formulas, a spelling, headings in sentence case, a closing section that explains the filing, and sources at the end. A request for "nine paragraphs" promises nine. Formally, a context $$c$$ induces a set of obligations $$\Phi(c) = \{\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_k\}$$, and an output $$y$$ is coherent with $$c$$ when

$$
y \models \varphi_i \quad \text{for every } \varphi_i \in \Phi(c).
$$

The obligations come in a few shapes, and linear temporal logic names most of them. Amir Pnueli introduced the logic for reasoning about programs in 1977; De Giacomo and Vardi gave it its semantics on finite traces in 2013, and a text is a finite trace.

<div class="cx-text">

| shape | formula | in this notebook | how it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| invariant | $$\mathbf{G}\,p$$, always $$p$$ | every formula uses the double-dollar delimiter | at any step: a slip, then compounding |
| eventuality | $$\mathbf{F}\,q$$, eventually $$q$$ | some section explains the complexity class | only at the end: stopping unpaid |
| order | $$\lnot r \;\mathbf{U}\; q$$, no $$r$$ until $$q$$ | that section comes before the sources | when the second arrives first |
| last | $$\mathbf{F}\,r \land \mathbf{G}(r \to \mathbf{G}\,s)$$ | once the sources begin, only sources follow | as eventuality, then as invariant |
| count | $$\#p = n$$, bounded | the requested number of paragraphs | at the end, if nothing counted |

</div>

The last row is not a temporal operator, but counting to a fixed bound is still something a finite automaton can do. The last column is the point of the table: each shape fails through a different mechanism, and the next section proves one result per mechanism.

All of these can be checked while the text is written. Bauer, Leucker and Schallhart (2011) give a three-valued verdict on a prefix $$u$$ of a run: $$\top$$ if every continuation satisfies $$\varphi$$, $$\bot$$ if none does, and $$?$$ otherwise. A text ends, and at its end every verdict is settled. So an invariant can only move from $$?$$ to $$\bot$$, and is kept if the text ends unbroken; an eventuality can only move from $$?$$ to $$\top$$, and is broken if the text ends unpaid. Each obligation compiles to a small deterministic automaton that reads the output once, keeping a few bits of state as it goes.

### What a transformer can and cannot do with them

These are regular languages, the bottom rung of the hierarchy Chomsky set out in 1956 and 1959. A finite automaton keeps them with a constant amount of state. Keith Duggar has made the matching point about networks on Machine Learning Street Talk: a network of fixed size is a finite-state machine, not a Turing machine. There is also a theorem that says exactly how well transformers fit. Yang, Chiang and Angluin proved that transformers with *hard* attention (each position attends to exactly one other) and a strict causal mask recognise exactly the languages definable in linear temporal logic; Yang and Chiang compiled a temporal logic with counting into ordinary softmax transformers. So every obligation above can be written as a transformer. And Delétang and colleagues, training 20,910 networks on 15 tasks grouped by the Chomsky hierarchy, found that what lets a network carry such structure to longer inputs is an explicit memory it can update, a stack or a tape.

Put these together with Fact 2. The idealised transformer can express the obligations, but it has no state to keep them in, so it must re-express them at every step through attention. With hard attention that costs nothing: one position, found exactly. With the soft attention we actually train, each re-derivation is paid for from the shared budget of Fact 1. That is where the failures come from.

### Competence and performance

In *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965), Chomsky separated what a speaker knows, *competence*, from what a speaker does, *performance*. Linguistic theory, he wrote, studies an ideal speaker

> unaffected by such grammatically irrelevant conditions as memory limitations, distractions, shifts of attention and interest, and errors (random or characteristic) in applying his knowledge of the language in actual performance.

The expressivity results above are a competence statement; the three failures below are performance, and they fall under exactly Chomsky's headings: memory limitations (no state), distractions (the nearest text), shifts of attention (dilution). The same split shows in practice. Methods that ask a model to criticise and revise its own draft, such as Self-Refine (Madaan et al. 2023), improve the draft, which they could not do if the model could not see what was wrong. Checking is a competence the model has. Subbarao Kambhampati, on Machine Learning Street Talk, draws the practical conclusion: let the model propose and let external verifiers check, in a loop he calls LLM-Modulo. The Ledger moves the verifiers inside. They supervise the network during training, and a small state carries their verdicts while it writes. Chomsky has been a severe critic of language models; he discussed them on Machine Learning Street Talk in 2022, and in the *New York Times* in 2023 he and his co-authors called ChatGPT "a lumbering statistical engine for pattern matching". This post does not need to settle that argument. It borrows his distinction because it says where to look: not at what the model knows, but at the machinery that carries what it knows through a long performance.

## Three ways to lose the thread

### Dilution

**Lemma 1.** Suppose every attention score in a row satisfies $$|s_j| \le B$$. Then every weight satisfies

$$
a_j = \frac{e^{s_j}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} e^{s_i}} \le \frac{e^{B}}{n\, e^{-B}} = \frac{e^{2B}}{n}.
$$

*Proof.* The numerator is at most $$e^B$$ and each of the $$n$$ terms of the denominator is at least $$e^{-B}$$. ∎

So the $$m$$ tokens of an instruction receive at most $$m e^{2B}/n$$ of a head's attention, and their contribution to its output is at most that fraction of the largest value vector. While the model writes, $$n = n_0 + t$$ grows by one with every token. The lemma is an upper bound: a model can stay below it and still work, but it cannot hold a fixed instruction's share unless its scores grow. How fast they must grow is exact.

**Corollary 1.** Let $$m$$ instruction tokens have score $$\Delta$$ above the other $$n - m$$ tokens, which share a common score. Their share of the head is at least $$c \in (0, 1)$$ if and only if

$$
\Delta \ \ge\ \log \frac{c}{1 - c} + \log \frac{n - m}{m}.
$$

*Proof.* The share is $$a = m e^{\Delta} / (m e^{\Delta} + n - m)$$. Rearranging $$a \ge c$$ gives $$e^{\Delta} \ge \frac{c}{1-c} \cdot \frac{n - m}{m}$$. ∎

The edge an instruction needs grows like $$\log n$$. A network whose scores are bounded by $$B$$ has an edge of at most $$2B$$, so its instruction falls below any share $$c$$ once $$n > m\bigl(1 + e^{2B}(1-c)/c\bigr)$$, a fixed length set by the weights. The corollary also explains the known remedies. Veličković and colleagues proved that softmax attention must disperse as inputs grow; Nakanishi's scalable softmax multiplies every score by $$s \log n$$, which supplies exactly the logarithmic growth the corollary asks for; trained models park spare attention on the first few tokens, the attention sinks of Xiao and colleagues; and Federico Barbero's "Transformers need glasses!", discussed on Machine Learning Street Talk, shows the end result, distinct inputs whose final representations become almost identical. With rotary positions there is a second tax on top: Su and colleagues showed that rotary attention decays with relative distance, so an instruction at the start of a long context is penalised for the size of the crowd and for its distance from it.

Scalable softmax grows the edge like log n, as Corollary 1 requires. The Ledger's read is normalised over its own K slots, so its share does not depend on the length of the context at all."></div>

*What would fix it:* a channel whose normalisation does not include the context.

### Compounding

An invariant must hold at every opportunity to break it. Let $$h_i(t)$$ be the probability that invariant $$i$$ breaks for the first time at step $$t$$, given that it has held so far: its *hazard*.

**Proposition 2.** Treating $$k$$ invariants as independent, the probability that all of them survive $$L$$ tokens is

$$
S(L) = \prod_{i=1}^{k} \prod_{t=1}^{L} \bigl(1 - h_i(t)\bigr) \le \exp\!\Bigl(-\sum_{i=1}^{k} \sum_{t=1}^{L} h_i(t)\Bigr).
$$

Suppose further that each hazard is a non-increasing function of the attention share its evidence receives, $$h_i(t) = g_i(\text{share}_i(t))$$, with $$g_i$$ continuous at 0 and $$g_i(0) = h_i^{\infty} > 0$$. Then with bounded scores

$$
\limsup_{L \to \infty} \frac{1}{L} \log S(L) \le -\sum_{i=1}^{k} h_i^{\infty}:
$$

the chance of keeping every invariant falls exponentially with length, at a rate that adds up over the invariants.

*Proof.* The product form is the chain rule for first-failure times, and the bound is $$1 - x \le e^{-x}$$. By Lemma 1, $$\text{share}_i(t) \le m_i e^{2B}/(n_0 + t) \to 0$$, so by continuity $$h_i(t) \to h_i^{\infty}$$, and the running average $$\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t \le L} h_i(t)$$ converges to the same limit. Divide the logarithm of the bound by $$L$$ and let $$L$$ grow. ∎

The same arithmetic lies behind the collapse Dziri and colleagues measured in multi-step problems, where small per-step error rates multiply. A long post is a many-step problem, and every paragraph is a step for every invariant at once:

<div data-survival data-caption="The chance that all k obligations survive. Each one's hazard either stays at its opening value, as if attention to the prompt never faded, or climbs towards p∞ as the prompt recedes, with τ setting how fast. Over a thousand words, small rates add up."></div>

Two predictions follow, and the experiment tests both. First, *per opportunity to break*, a sparse invariant should be more fragile than a dense one. An invariant exercised on every line, like the font of each paragraph, can be re-read from the model's own recent output; one exercised rarely, like the delimiter of a formula that turns up once every few hundred words, must be recovered from the examples each time, across a widening gap. (A dense invariant can still fail more often *per post*, because it gets more chances.) Second, the product form should predict whole-post failure rates from per-opportunity hazards, including the rate at which all invariants survive together.

*What would fix it:* carry the obligation as state, so that each step reads it instead of re-deriving it. Better still, where possible, remove the choice altogether; the section on symmetry shows when that is possible.

### Blind stopping

Eventualities are judged only at the end, and the end is itself a token, $$\langle\text{eos}\rangle$$.

**Proposition 3.** Suppose that at each step while an eventuality $$q$$ is owed, the model ends the text with probability $$e$$, and otherwise pays $$q$$ with probability $$d$$. Then

$$
P(\text{the text ends with } q \text{ unpaid}) = \sum_{t \ge 0} e\,\bigl((1 - e)(1 - d)\bigr)^{t} = \frac{e}{e + d - e d}.
$$

*Proof.* The text ends unpaid at step $$t$$ if it neither ended nor paid at each earlier step, which has probability $$((1-e)(1-d))^t$$, and then ends. Sum the geometric series. ∎

With time-varying rates the same sum holds term by term. The probability of ending unpaid is governed by the ratio of the stop rate to the pay rate, and nothing in a transformer ties the first to what is owed: the decision to stop is one entry of one softmax, computed from the same diluted state as everything else. Two less-cited results show how fragile that entry is. Stahlberg and Byrne searched translation models exactly and found that for more than half of all sentences the single most probable output was the empty one: the models prefer to stop. Newman, Hewitt, Liang and Manning found that training a model to predict the end token at all makes it generalise worse to longer outputs, because its hidden states organise themselves around position.

*What would fix it:* make the stop rate depend on the debt, so that $$e$$ falls while $$q$$ is owed.

## The Ledger

The three fixes are one design. The Ledger gives the obligations a state (against compounding, and to count with), reads that state through a softmax of its own (against dilution), and lets the state hold back the end of the text (against blind stopping). It sits beside an ordinary decoder and changes nothing about it:

<LedgerDiagram />

**The clerk.** Once the prompt has been read, $$K$$ learned queries attend over the prompt's hidden states at a middle layer $$\ell_c$$, and write $$K$$ slot vectors $$o_1, \dots, o_K$$: what is owed. The clerk runs once per prompt, which is how implicit rules get in: it reads them off the examples, and small linear heads on each slot (which font, whether sources are expected, how many paragraphs) are where training can check what it read.

**The state.** This is the part the transformer lacks (Fact 2). Every written token updates a scalar per slot. With $$h_t$$ the token's layer-normalised state at layer $$\ell_c$$, the event probability is $$e_{t,i} = \sigma\bigl(\langle W_h h_t,\, W_o o_i\rangle/\sqrt{d} + b_i\bigr)$$, and the status $$u_{t,i}$$ accumulates it according to the slot's type, which is the automaton of the previous section written in differentiable form:

$$
u_{t,i} = \begin{cases}
0 & \text{invariant: always in force} \\[2pt]
1 - \prod_{s \le t} (1 - e_{s,i}) & \text{eventuality: noisy-OR, monotone} \\[2pt]
\sigma\bigl(4\,(\textstyle\sum_{s \le t} e_{s,i} - \hat n_i + \tfrac12)\bigr) & \text{count, with target } \hat n_i \text{ read by the clerk}
\end{cases}
$$

An eventuality or count is *pending* with weight $$\pi_{t,i} = r_i (1 - u_{t,i})$$, where $$r_i$$ is the clerk's estimate that it applies at all; an invariant is never pending, only in force.

**The read.** In every layer above $$\ell_c$$, each written token attends to the $$K$$ slots, whose keys and values are built from $$o_i$$ and from $$(u_{t,i}, \pi_{t,i}, r_i)$$. This softmax, one per head as in multi-head attention, is normalised over the $$K$$ slots only, never over the context (Fact 1 no longer applies to it):

$$
x_t \leftarrow x_t + \tanh(g_\ell)\; W_O \sum_{i=1}^{K} \mathrm{softmax}_i\!\left(\frac{q_t \cdot k_{t,i}}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right) v_{t,i}.
$$

**The gate.** Pending eventualities and counts hold back the end of the text, with $$\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$$:

$$
\text{logit}_t(\langle\text{eos}\rangle) \mathrel{+}= \gamma \sum_{i \,\in\, \text{eventualities, counts}} \log\bigl(1 - \pi_{t,i} + \varepsilon\bigr).
$$

While a debt is fully owed ($$\pi = 1$$), this multiplies the odds of stopping by $$\varepsilon^{\gamma} = 10^{-3\gamma}$$, which is exactly the lever Proposition 3 asks for. The toy learned $$\gamma$$ between 0.30 and 0.42 across runs: the odds of stopping with a debt outstanding are cut by a factor of 8 to 18.

**Training.** Checking is cheap, so the checkers can teach. Next to the usual next-token loss, the clerk's heads learn from labels for each obligation, and the state learns from the monitors' verdicts on every prefix of the training text: whether the sources have begun yet, and, for a count, whether a paragraph starts here. Every training token becomes a supervised example of bookkeeping.

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{LM}} + \lambda_{\text{ext}} \sum_i \mathrm{CE}(\text{clerk}_i, \varphi_i) + \lambda_{\text{ev}} \sum_{t, i} \mathrm{BCE}\bigl(u_{t,i},\, [\![ y_{\le t} \text{ has paid } \varphi_i ]\!]\bigr).
$$

### What the Ledger buys

Five properties follow from the definitions.

1. **It changes nothing when inserted.** With $$g_\ell = 0$$ and $$\gamma = 0$$, $$\tanh(g_\ell) = 0$$ and the gate adds 0, so the network computes exactly what it computed before; a trained model can be extended without being disturbed. The zero-initialised tanh gate is Flamingo's device for grafting new inputs onto a frozen model. In the toy, every placement tested below matched the baseline's loss to 12 decimals at insertion.
2. **Its share does not dilute.** The read is normalised over $$K$$ slots, so by the argument of Lemma 1 a slot with a score edge $$\Delta$$ over the others gets at least $$e^{\Delta}/(e^{\Delta} + K - 1)$$ of the read, whatever $$n$$ and $$t$$ are. Corollary 1's logarithmic growth is no longer needed.
3. **Paid stays paid.** Eventuality and count status never decrease in $$t$$ (a product of factors in $$[0,1]$$ can only shrink; a sum of non-negative terms can only grow), like the $$\top$$ of a monitor, so the gate cannot trap the model once the debt is settled.
4. **It trains in parallel.** The noisy-OR is a cumulative sum of $$\log(1 - e_{s,i})$$ and the count a cumulative sum of $$e_{s,i}$$, so training needs no back-propagation through time, even though the state is a recurrence.
5. **It is cheap, and it is readable.** A slot's state is $$o_i$$ plus a linear function of three numbers, so its keys and values are $$W o_i$$, computed once per prompt, plus a rank-3 correction. Per written token and read layer that leaves one query projection and $$K$$ dot products, $$O(d^2 + Kd)$$, with no term in $$n$$; self-attention costs $$O(d^2 + nd)$$. And the slots can be read out: what the clerk believes is owed, and what the model believes it has paid.

The advantages are clearest against the alternatives, each of which fixes part of the problem:

## Symmetry: when the state collapses

The Ledger keeps an invariant alive with a state and a read, which lowers its hazard; it cannot make it zero. For one large class of invariants, more is possible, and mathematics has a name for the reason.

### Coherence in mathematics

Mathematics has used the word *coherence* for a long time, with a precise meaning. In a monoidal category there are many ways to rebracket a product such as $$(a \otimes b) \otimes (c \otimes d)$$, each a chain of associativity isomorphisms. Mac Lane's coherence theorem (1963) says that any two such chains between the same two bracketings are equal: every diagram built from them commutes. An equivalent form says that every monoidal category is equivalent to a *strict* one, in which those isomorphisms are identities and there is nothing left to choose. Coherence, there, is the guarantee that choices made in different places agree, and strictification makes the agreement automatic.

Many conventions are choices of exactly this kind: among interchangeable variants, which font, which delimiter, "colour" or "color". The text keeps such a convention when it makes the same choice everywhere. The Ledger helps the network remember its choice. Strictification suggests something better: make the choice once, outside the network, and leave nothing to remember.

### A group acting on words

Let a finite group $$G$$ act on the vocabulary $$V$$ by permutations, $$\rho : G \to \mathrm{Sym}(V)$$ with $$\rho(gh) = \rho(g)\,\rho(h)$$, extended to texts one token at a time. In the toy below, $$G = S_6 \times S_4 \times S_3$$: one factor permutes the six fonts, one the four heading styles, one the three delimiters (opening and closing together), and every other token is fixed. The orbits $$G \cdot v = \{\rho(g)\,v : g \in G\}$$ are the four style families plus single tokens, and a style is a choice of one member from each family. Attention has a symmetry of the same kind, permutations, acting on positions instead of words: without positional encodings and without the mask it is equivariant to any reordering of the tokens, which is why the 2017 paper had to add positions. The Geometric Deep Learning programme of Bronstein, Bruna, Cohen and Veličković, the subject of a special edition of Machine Learning Street Talk, builds whole architectures from such symmetries; Gordon, Lopez-Paz, Baroni and Bouchacourt, and Akyürek and Andreas, used groups acting on words for compositional generalisation.

Write $$C(x, q)$$ for the set of coherent posts given example posts $$x$$ and a request $$q$$.

**Proposition 4 (equivariance).** For every $$g \in G$$, $$C(\rho(g)\,x,\ q) = \rho(g)\,C(x, q)$$. Relabel the author's examples, and the coherent posts are relabelled the same way.

*Proof.* Each style monitor checks that every token of its family equals the member the examples use; applying one permutation to both sides preserves that equality. The other monitors never ask which member of a family appears, and from the request they read only the requested length, which $$G$$ fixes. ∎

A network that learns this relation from data must learn it separately for every style it meets, and it sees the rare styles least. In the toy's baseline (two seeds, 600 posts), authors who use the default font had it kept 96.7% of the time, and authors who use one of the other five, 91.2%; for delimiters, 97.2% and 90.4%. The symmetry is in the task and missing from the model.

### Gauge fixing

In physics, a gauge is a choice of reference frame that the laws do not depend on, and fixing it removes the redundancy. Choose a canonical style $$c$$ (the first member of each family). Read the author's style $$\hat s$$ from the examples, and let $$\hat g$$ be a group element with $$\hat g \cdot c = \hat s$$; in the toy, a product of three transpositions, which is its own inverse. Then:

1. move the context into the canonical frame, $$x \mapsto \rho(\hat g)^{-1} x$$;
2. let the transformer, trained the same way in the canonical frame, write with its output restricted to the canonical member of every family, so that its alphabet is the set of orbits $$V/G$$ (96 tokens become 84);
3. map the text back, $$y = \rho(\hat g)\, y_c$$.

<FrameDiagram />

**Proposition 5 (invariants by construction).** Under gauge fixing, the invariant of family $$i$$ holds at every position of the text if the frame read it correctly, and fails at every occurrence of that family otherwise. For every length $$L$$,

$$
P(\text{invariant } i \text{ is broken}) \le P(\hat s_i \ne s_i),
$$

with equality whenever the family occurs at all.

*Proof.* The only member of family $$i$$ the network can emit is $$c_i$$, and step 3 maps it to $$\rho(\hat g)\,c_i = \hat s_i$$ wherever it appears. So every occurrence matches the author if $$\hat s_i = s_i$$, and none does otherwise. ∎

**Proposition 6 (reading the frame).** Suppose the examples contain $$m$$ tokens of family $$i$$, each equal to the author's member independently with probability $$1 - \eta$$, where $$\eta < \tfrac12$$. Then the most frequent member is the author's with probability at least $$1 - e^{-2m(1/2 - \eta)^2}$$.

*Proof.* Let $$X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(m, 1 - \eta)$$ count the author's tokens. If $$X > m/2$$ the author's member is a strict majority. By Hoeffding's inequality, $$P(X \le m/2) = P\bigl(X - m(1 - \eta) \le -m(\tfrac12 - \eta)\bigr) \le e^{-2m(1/2 - \eta)^2}$$. ∎

Together they give the result the section is for.

**Theorem 1 (coherence that does not decay with length).** Under gauge fixing, with the frame read by majority count and the assumptions of Proposition 6 for each family $$i$$ (with $$m_i$$ examples and noise $$\eta_i$$), the probability that every group-structured invariant holds throughout a text of any length $$L$$ is at least

$$
1 - \sum_i e^{-2 m_i (1/2 - \eta_i)^2}.
$$

*Proof.* By Proposition 5 the text keeps all these invariants whenever every family's frame is read correctly, whatever $$L$$ is. By Proposition 6 each family is misread with probability at most $$e^{-2 m_i (1/2 - \eta_i)^2}$$. A union bound over the families gives the result. ∎

Compare Proposition 2. There, the chance of keeping $$k$$ invariants falls exponentially with length, at a rate set by their hazards. Here, the bound does not mention $$L$$ at all: the whole risk sits in one reading, made before the first word is written, and it shrinks exponentially with the number of examples rather than growing with the length of the text. This is strictification in miniature. The many places that had to agree are replaced by one choice that cannot disagree.

### What it saves

Gauge fixing also saves work. Once the clerk and the frame have read the examples, the written tokens need the examples only for their content. When the examples are there for their conventions alone, as in the toy, their keys and values can be dropped as soon as they are read: each written token then attends to the request, its own text and the $$K$$ slots, and a post of $$L$$ tokens that followed $$n_{\text{ex}}$$ example tokens saves $$L\, n_{\text{ex}}$$ key lookups per head and layer. Gist tokens (Mu et al. 2023) compress prompts the same way, by masking attention during training; here what survives the compression is typed and checked. The output alphabet also shrinks from $$V$$ to $$V/G$$, and the network stops spending capacity on relabelled copies of the same behaviour.

There are limits. The group must act on the units the model writes: "colour" and "color" can be tokenised differently, and lower-casing a heading is not invertible, so not every convention is a permutation of tokens. Examples that carry content, such as voice, topic and facts, cannot be dropped wholesale; only their conventions can be factored out. And obligations with no symmetry, like "end with sources" or "write nine paragraphs", stay with the Ledger.

## Where coherence belongs

Each part of the repair answers one failure, and each failure happens in one place in the network, so placement is not a free choice.

Start from the residual stream. Elhage and colleagues describe a transformer as a shared channel that every attention layer and feed-forward layer reads from and adds to; the logits read its final sum. Anything written into the stream at any depth reaches the output directly. What depends on the depth is what the rest of the network can do with it.

**Before: in the context.** Written as tokens in the prompt, obligations compete in the same softmax as everything else (Lemma 1), and with rotary positions they also sit ever further back. This is the placement Corollary 1 rules out.

**Inside, before or after attention.** Added to the stream just before a layer's attention, the Ledger's read can change what that attention looks up ("sources are still owed" becomes "find how the examples wrote their sources"). Added after, it reaches the feed-forward layer and the layers above. Either way the network can compute with it.

**Inside, as modulation.** The ledger can scale and shift the normalised stream instead of being read by attention. Peebles and Xie compared exactly these options for conditioning diffusion transformers: tokens in the context, cross-attention, and zero-initialised adaptive layer norm. The last gave the best images at the lowest cost, and tokens in the context the worst.

**After: at the output.** Biasing the logits, gating $$\langle\text{eos}\rangle$$ or restricting the alphabet can veto or choose among tokens, exactly, but cannot make the network retrieve or plan anything.

**Across: along the sequence.** Attention keeps no state (Fact 2). The obligations are regular languages, whose natural carrier is a finite state updated as the text goes by. The Ledger's status is exactly that: a small recurrence along the positions, beside the attention rather than inside it.

The rule that follows: act on an obligation at the lowest place where it can be both checked and acted on, and no lower.

<div class="cx-text">

| obligation | where it breaks | where to act |
|---|---|---|
| a choice among interchangeable variants | one output token | after: restrict the alphabet (the frame) |
| an eventuality or a count | the decision to stop | across: a status; inside: a read of it; after: a gate on stopping |
| an order, or anything that needs content retrieved | what is looked up next | inside, before attention |
| voice, topic, facts | everywhere | inside, by ordinary attention |

</div>

The second row carries a correction from the experiment: a count needs its status read inside the network, not only a gate at the end.

## An experiment

A claim about architecture should be tested where everything else is held still. Frontier models cannot be retrained here, so I built the smallest world that has the problem, and designed it so that each numbered result above has a measurement that could have come out against it.

### The task

An *author* has a house style: one of six paragraph fonts, one of four heading styles, one of three formula delimiters, and two habits, a closing "why" section and a sources section, each present or absent. The context shows two or three of the author's posts, then a request: a line in the *default* style (plain serif, single-dollar formulas) that says how many paragraphs to write. Then the model writes. The style is never stated. It has to be read off the posts and held against the request, which sits right next to the point where writing begins. The words themselves come from a fixed random chain over 48 words and mean nothing. Chomsky's ideas are colourless on purpose: only form is under test.

Most authors in the training data use the default style (40% of fonts, half of all delimiters), so a model that stops looking at the evidence drifts towards the request's style. Seven monitors check each post for the implicit rules: font, headings and maths (the three invariants), the why-section and the sources (eventualities, with sources required to come last), their order, and form (no token that belongs to the prompt, such as a stray request marker). An eighth checks the requested length, the one explicit instruction and the one count.

### The models

Six variants, identical except for the parts under test: a 3-layer decoder with 4 heads, width 64 and rotary positions (155,456 parameters), trained from scratch for 1,500 steps on fresh batches of 32 episodes, with AdamW (peak learning rate 0.002, cosine decay, gradient clipping at 1) and the loss taken only on the post the model writes. The Ledger's two label losses are weighted 0.3 each.

1. **baseline**: the plain decoder.
2. **baseline + same labels**: the plain decoder with the Ledger's supervision attached to linear probes on its hidden states. If the Ledger wins only because it sees more labels, this variant will win too.
3. **Ledger**: clerk at layer 1, reads after attention in layers 2 and 3, $$K = 8$$ slots (three invariants, two eventualities, one count, two left free and unsupervised), the gate on the eventuality and count slots, and supervision (212,427 parameters).
4. **Ledger, one shared softmax**: the same slots, read inside the ordinary attention softmax, as tokens in the context would be. This isolates Fact 1: same state, same labels, shared budget.
5. **Ledger + frame**: the Ledger with gauge fixing.
6. **Ledger + frame, examples dropped**: as 5, but written tokens may not attend to the example posts; only the clerk and the frame read them, so their keys and values can be discarded before writing begins.

Each trained model wrote 300 posts, one for each of the same 300 prompts (two example posts each; 5 requested lengths, 60 authors each), sampled at temperature 1 so that the numbers describe the model's own distribution rather than a decoding trick. Because every model sees the same prompts, pairs of models can be compared prompt by prompt with an exact McNemar test.

**The compute behind it.** Everything here ran on four CPU cores, with no accelerator. One run at the protocol above takes about twenty minutes to train and ten to evaluate, so I capped the experiment at what fits in that budget and say so rather than stretch it. The baseline and the Ledger were trained with two seeds each; the labelled baseline, the shared-softmax Ledger and the two Ledgers with a frame with one seed each; the remaining ablations did not fit, and are listed at the end as work to do. The placement question got a cheaper screen: all six placements and the baseline, one seed each, trained for 400 steps instead of 1,500 and scored on 100 prompts. A screen ranks candidates; it does not settle them.

### Results, claim by claim

Across 600 posts from two seeds, the baseline kept all seven implicit rules in 80.5% of them (77.3% and 83.7% for the two seeds). The Ledger kept them in 90.0% (89.0% and 91.0%). On the same prompts and seeds, the Ledger succeeded where the baseline failed 84 times, and the reverse happened 27 times; the exact McNemar test gives $$p = 5.5 \times 10^{-8}$$. With the frame the Ledger reached 95.0%, and with the examples dropped 93.7% (one seed each; $$p = 3 \times 10^{-6}$$ and $$1 \times 10^{-9}$$ against the baseline on the same prompts).

## Putting it in a real model

The recipe for a pretrained model follows the toy's. First the symmetry: declare the families of interchangeable variants the model writes (delimiters, quotation marks, list markers, spelling pairs that tokenise alike), read the frame by counting them in the examples, and restrict the output to one member per family. This part needs no training at all. Then the Ledger: freeze the model, insert the clerk a third of the way up and reads in the layers above, with $$K$$ between 16 and 32 and every gate at zero, so that on the first step nothing has changed. Train the new parameters, optionally with low-rank adapters on the old ones, on instruction and imitation data that comes with programmatic monitors: the verifiable instructions of IFEval, and style monitors extracted from the examples themselves, like the ones in the audit below. Match slots to monitors per example by bipartite matching, as DETR does for objects, since there are more kinds of obligation than slots. At inference the cost is a few $$K \times d$$ products per layer and token, and the key-value cache is untouched.

The theory makes predictions that such a test should check. The shared-softmax version should degrade as the context grows, and the separate one should not (Corollary 1). With a Ledger, the hazard of a sparse invariant should stop rising with length (Proposition 2). For conventions that are genuine permutations of the vocabulary, the violation rate should be independent of length and equal to the rate at which the frame is misread (Theorem 1). And dropping the examples should cost nothing on obligations the Ledger and the frame carry, while saving attention in proportion to the length of the examples.

Its symmetry is exact and declared in advance; real conventions are only approximately permutations of tokens. The experiments that should come next, in order:

1. The same toy with more seeds and longer training, and the ablations the budget left out: the Ledger without its gate, the Ledger without monitor labels, and the frame without a Ledger.
2. The retrofit described above, on a pretrained open model, with the IFEval checkers and style monitors extracted from examples, measured on IFEval, Multi-IF, IFScale and a blog-imitation set like Wang et al.'s.
3. Contexts of 32,000 tokens and more, where Lemma 1 bites hardest and dropping the examples saves the most.
4. Symmetries that are learned rather than declared, in the spirit of LexSym, for conventions that are only nearly permutations.

## The ledger of this notebook

The monitors are the easy half of all this, and the easy half is useful on its own: they are the same kind of checker that supervised the Ledger during training. One rule is owed regardless. ## Why the class is NP

Checking whether a draft keeps a set of obligations takes one pass of a few small automata: time linear in the length of the draft. Writing a draft that keeps them all while also being worth reading is a search through an enormous space, and nothing makes it easy. A proposed answer is easy to verify, a good one is hard to find, and that shape is NP, the same shape as running Life backwards in [Gliders are messages](/posts/gliders-are-messages/). The Ledger's bet is that the easy side can teach the hard one, during training, one token at a time; the frame's is that, for some obligations, the hard side can be made to disappear.
