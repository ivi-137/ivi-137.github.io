Here is an experiment anyone can do. Give a language model the posts of this notebook and ask it for the next one, nine paragraphs long. For a while the result is good. The topic is right and the voice is close. Then, somewhere around the fourth paragraph, a formula turns up between single dollar signs, although every formula here sits between two. A heading arrives in Title Case. "Behaviour" loses its u. And the post stops after seven paragraphs, without the list of sources that closes every post in this notebook.

None of these mistakes is hard to avoid. The model knows how to type two dollar signs. It has seen hundreds of them in this very context, and if you asked, it could point to every one. So what went wrong? This post is my attempt to answer that properly, with proofs where proofs are possible and an experiment where they are not.

Five promises were broken in that draft, and I will follow each of them through the whole post:

1. **the double dollars**: every formula sits between two dollar signs;
2. **sentence case**: every heading is written like a sentence;
3. **the u in behaviour**: the spelling is British;
4. **nine paragraphs**: the length that was asked for;
5. **sources last**: every post ends with its sources.

Before any mathematics, a thought experiment. Imagine a scribe copying out a long letter in a dark room. The scribe's memory for the page is perfect, in a sense. Any word already on it can be looked at again, as often as needed. But there is one lamp, and its light is fixed. To read the instructions at the top of the page, the scribe must turn the lamp that way, and whatever light falls on the top is taken from everything else. Each new line adds more page for the same light. And the scribe keeps no notes in the margin, so to know whether the sources have been listed yet, or how many paragraphs are done, the only way is to look again, by lamplight, every time.

That scribe is a fair picture of a transformer. The lamp is the softmax inside attention, whose weights always add up to one. The missing margin is the fact that a transformer carries nothing from one step to the next except the words themselves. Almost everything in this post follows from those two facts.

The question I want to answer is not whether the scribe makes mistakes, because everyone does. It is how fast the chance of a flawless letter falls as the letter gets longer. Call that chance $$S(L)$$ for a text of $$L$$ tokens. For long texts it falls like $$e^{-\kappa L}$$, and the number $$\kappa$$, which I will call the *length exponent*, is what I want to understand. A small $$\kappa$$ means a long text stays faithful. A large one means it rots.

Here is what I found. In an ordinary transformer, $$\kappa$$ drifts towards the value it would have if the instructions were never looked at at all, and a longer prompt does not change where it ends up. Give the promises a small state of their own, read through a softmax that sums over the promises instead of over the whole text, and $$\kappa$$ stays below a bound that depends on neither the prompt nor the length of the text. For one special kind of promise, a choice between interchangeable variants such as one dollar sign or two, $$\kappa$$ can be made exactly zero, by making the choice once, before the first word is written. The end of a text fails in a way of its own, through the decision to stop, and that gets its own formula.

I call the module that does this the *Ledger*. In a small, controlled experiment, trained on four CPU cores, it raised the share of posts that kept every implicit rule from 80.5% to 90.0% ($$p = 5.5 \times 10^{-8}$$), and the share with the right number of paragraphs from 38.5% to 98.5%. The same slots squeezed into the model's ordinary softmax gave nothing (74.0%). With the choice of style fixed once, not one of 4,526 chances to break a convention was taken.

That experiment is a toy, and I will say so each time it matters. The proofs carry the weight of the argument, and the toy checks that the mechanisms behave the way the proofs say. The test on a real pretrained model is built and tested, and waiting for its GPU hours. Its predictions are written down below, before any result exists, so that I cannot move the target afterwards.

A word about the formulas. I have tried to make each one earn its place, but if one gets in your way, skip it. The sentences around every formula say what it means, and you can come back to it later.

## How often models lose the thread

One bad afternoon with one model is not much to build a theory on, so the first thing to ask is how common this is. Nobody has measured coherence exactly as I will define it. Three kinds of study come close: instructions that a program can check, very long inputs, and imitation of a writing style. Here is what they found.

<div class="cx-text">

| study | what was measured | what they found |
|---|---|---|
| IFEval, Zhou et al. 2023 | about 500 prompts with 25 kinds of verifiable instruction, such as "no commas" or "at least 3 sections" | GPT-4 kept every instruction of a prompt 76.9% of the time: roughly one prompt in four broke something a program could check |
| Multi-IF, He et al. 2024 | instructions of the same kind over three turns, in eight languages | o1-preview fell from 0.877 at the first turn to 0.707 at the third |

Sort the rows by what makes things worse and three groups appear. In some studies the failures grow with the length of the context: RULER, NoLiMa, lost in the middle, the persona drift of Li and colleagues, the padded questions of Levy and colleagues. In others they grow with the number of rules or turns, as in IFEval, Multi-IF, IFScale and the conversations of Laban and colleagues. And some happen at the very end of the text, above all the missing citations of ALCE. Keep the three groups in mind. The theory below produces exactly three mechanisms, and they match the groups one to one. Imitation from examples, the hardest case, suffers from all three and from a fourth difficulty besides, because nobody tells the model what the rules are.

Two things in the table strike me most. Laban and colleagues separated *aptitude*, how well a model does a task at its best, from *reliability*, how often it does it that well. When a task was spread over several turns, aptitude barely moved and reliability collapsed. The models could do the work; they just didn't do it every time. That is the signature of a mechanism, not of missing knowledge. The other is how stable the pattern is. Newer models do better on every row, yet each of these studies, across labs and years, found failures that grow with length, turns or the number of rules. When the same shape turns up everywhere, it is worth asking what everything has in common. These models have one thing in common above all, a design published in 2017.

## The machine

So let us look at that design, the Transformer, the way its first paper built it. I will go through it with one purpose, which is to find where a promise could live inside it. Three parts matter later. The softmax gives Fact 1, the way the model writes gives Fact 2, and the residual stream decides where a repair can go.

### Tokens and positions

Ashish Vaswani and seven co-authors introduced the Transformer in 2017, to translate sentences. Text is cut into tokens, and each token becomes a vector of $$d_{\text{model}} = 512$$ numbers. There is no recurrence and no convolution, and that has a curious consequence: nothing in the model knows which token came first. Shuffle the words, and without extra help each of them would be processed exactly as before. So order has to be put in by hand. Each position $$p$$ gets a fixed vector, added to its token's:

$$
\mathrm{PE}(p, 2i) = \sin\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}, \qquad \mathrm{PE}(p, 2i+1) = \cos\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}.
$$

Picture each pair of dimensions as a clock hand, turning at its own speed, with periods from $$2\pi$$ to $$10000 \cdot 2\pi$$ positions. Clocks were a clever choice. Going from position $$p$$ to $$p + k$$ turns every hand by an angle that depends on $$k$$ alone, so the step is a rotation, the same for every $$p$$. As a result the dot product of two positions depends only on how far apart they are:

$$
\mathrm{PE}(p) \cdot \mathrm{PE}(p+k) = \sum_{i} \cos(\omega_i k), \qquad \omega_i = 10000^{-2i/d_{\text{model}}}.
$$

<div data-posenc data-caption="Sinusoidal positions: 96 positions by 128 dimensions, green positive and blue negative. Below, one position's similarity with all the others. Move the slider and the curve only shifts."></div>

Most models today use a descendant called rotary embeddings, which rotate the queries and keys instead of adding anything to the input, and the toy model later in this post does the same. Two things from this part will come back. Rotary attention fades with distance, which is a second tax on an instruction set far back. And without positions, attention cannot tell one order of the words from another. That is a symmetry, and the section on symmetry puts one to work.

### Attention

Each token produces three vectors, each a linear function of the token itself: a query $$q$$, a key $$k$$ and a value $$v$$. Stack them for all the tokens into matrices, and

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V.
$$

The formula is easier to understand one row at a time. One token's query is compared with every key by a dot product, which gives a score $$s_j$$ for each token $$j$$. The softmax turns the scores into weights, $$a_j = e^{s_j} / \sum_i e^{s_i}$$, and the output is the average of the values, taken with those weights. Every weight is positive, and the weights in a row always add up to exactly 1. That is the lamp from the dark room, and most of this post comes out of it.

Why divide by $$\sqrt{d_k}$$? If the components of $$q$$ and $$k$$ are independent, with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product has variance $$d_k$$ (the paper's footnote 4). Without the division the scores grow with the width of the head, the softmax piles all its weight on a single winner, and learning stalls because the gradients vanish. You can watch it happen:

<div data-attention data-caption="Scaled dot-product attention over one sentence. Click a word to make it the query. Widen d_k with the scaling switched off and the weights collapse onto one word."></div>

A model that writes from left to right must not peek at words it has not written yet, so the scores of later positions are set to $$-\infty$$ before the softmax. This is the causal mask.

### Heads, layers and training

The model runs $$h = 8$$ attentions side by side, each on its own 64-dimensional projections, and joins their results:

$$
\mathrm{MultiHead}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{Concat}(\mathrm{head}_1, \dots, \mathrm{head}_h)\, W^O, \qquad \mathrm{head}_i = \mathrm{Attention}(QW_i^Q, KW_i^K, VW_i^V).
$$

Each head is free to look for something different, such as the previous word, the subject of a verb or a matching bracket. After attention, every position goes on its own through a small two-layer network, $$\mathrm{FFN}(x) = \max(0,\, xW_1 + b_1)\,W_2 + b_2$$, with an inner width of 2048. Each of these parts is wrapped as $$\mathrm{LayerNorm}(x + \mathrm{Sublayer}(x))$$. The $$x +$$ is a residual connection, a way round the layer that lets information pass through untouched, and it matters later, because it is why a repair added at any depth can still reach the output. Six such layers make the encoder, and the decoder has six more, with a third part that attends to what the encoder produced.

The Transformer was built to translate one sentence at a time. Its inputs were a few dozen tokens long, one budget of attention was plenty, and nothing had to be remembered for long. The failures in this post appear when the same design is asked to write thousands of tokens against rules set far back.

The part of the paper I keep returning to is its Table 1, which compares kinds of layer:

| layer | cost per layer | sequential steps | longest path between two tokens |
|---|---|---|---|
| self-attention | $$O(n^2 d)$$ | $$O(1)$$ | $$O(1)$$ |
| recurrent | $$O(n d^2)$$ | $$O(n)$$ | $$O(n)$$ |
| convolutional | $$O(k n d^2)$$ | $$O(1)$$ | $$O(\log_k n)$$ |

In a recurrent network, a word at the start of a document has to survive $$n$$ updates before it can affect the end. In a transformer the path is one step long, because any token reads any other directly. That is a large part of why transformers replaced recurrent networks, and at first sight it seems to make forgetting impossible. It doesn't. A path of length one still has to be paid for, and the payment comes out of a budget that adds up to 1.

The language models in use today keep only the decoder. They are trained to predict the next token,

$$
p(y \mid c) = \prod_{t=1}^{L} p(y_t \mid c,\, y_{<t}),
$$

and they write by sampling one token at a time and feeding it back in.

### Two facts the rest depends on

Everything that follows is deduced from two facts about this design. I state them once, here, and refer back to them by number.

**Fact 1: one budget.** Every row of attention is a probability distribution over all the tokens it can see. Whatever a head gives to one token it takes from the others, and the number of others grows by one with every token written.

**Fact 2: memory without state.** Between two steps, a decoder carries nothing forward except the tokens themselves, together with their keys and values, which are fixed views of those tokens. It keeps no small running summary of the kind a finite automaton keeps, nothing like a note in the margin that the next step could inherit. So anything the text has committed to, a font, a count, a debt of sources, has to be found again at every step by attention over the whole context. This is the other side of the transformer's great strength. Because any token can read any other directly, the design never needed a margin, and so it has none.

The draft at the top of this post is both facts at work. The double dollars were set in the example posts, thousands of tokens before the fourth paragraph, and every token written since has joined the crowd they must be found in (Fact 1). And nothing was counting the paragraphs, or keeping track of the sources still owed (Fact 2).

## What coherence means

A promise can only be checked, or kept, once it has been said exactly. So this section turns the five promises into formulas. That pays off twice. A formula can be checked by a program, and later these programs become the teachers. And the formulas come in a few shapes, each of which breaks at its own moment, which is what the section after this one proves.

### Obligations

In 1957 Noam Chomsky wrote a sentence to make a point about grammar: "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously." Every word is in its place and the sentence means nothing. Being well formed and making sense are different properties, and coherence, in the sense I need, is a third one, closer to bookkeeping. A context makes promises about the text that follows it. The earlier posts in this notebook promise a way of writing formulas, a spelling, headings in sentence case, a closing section that explains where the post is filed, and sources at the end. A request for "nine paragraphs" promises nine. Formally, a context $$c$$ creates a set of obligations $$\Phi(c) = \{\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_k\}$$, and an output $$y$$ is coherent with $$c$$ when

$$
y \models \varphi_i \quad \text{for every } \varphi_i \in \Phi(c).
$$

The obligations come in a few shapes, and linear temporal logic has names for most of them. Amir Pnueli introduced the logic in 1977, to reason about programs that run for ever; De Giacomo and Vardi gave it a meaning on finite traces in 2013, and a text is a finite trace.

<div class="cx-text">

| shape | formula | in this notebook | how it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| invariant | $$\mathbf{G}\,p$$, always $$p$$ | the double dollars; sentence case; the u in behaviour | at any step: a slip, then compounding |
| eventuality | $$\mathbf{F}\,q$$, eventually $$q$$ | sources last: a sources section appears | only at the end: stopping unpaid |
| order | $$\lnot r \;\mathbf{U}\; q$$, no $$r$$ until $$q$$ | the section explaining the class comes before the sources | when the second arrives first |
| last | $$\mathbf{F}\,r \land \mathbf{G}(r \to \mathbf{G}\,s)$$ | once the sources begin, only sources follow | as eventuality, then as invariant |
| count | $$\#p = n$$, bounded | nine paragraphs | at the end, if nothing counted |

</div>

The count is not a temporal operator, but counting up to a fixed number is still something a finite automaton can do. Look at the last column. Each shape fails through a different mechanism, and that is the reason for sorting them.

All of these can be checked while the text is being written. Bauer, Leucker and Schallhart (2011) give a three-valued verdict on any prefix of a run: $$\top$$ if every continuation satisfies $$\varphi$$, $$\bot$$ if none does, and $$?$$ if it is too early to say. A text ends, and when it ends every verdict is settled. An invariant can only go from $$?$$ to $$\bot$$, and it is kept if the text ends unbroken. An eventuality can only go from $$?$$ to $$\top$$, and it is broken if the text ends unpaid. Each obligation compiles to a small automaton that reads the output once, keeping a few bits as it goes. Hold on to that picture of a monitor giving a verdict on every prefix, because a monitor like that can teach a network, token by token, to keep the same books.

### What a transformer can and cannot do with them

These languages sit on the bottom rung of the hierarchy Chomsky set out in 1956 and 1959, the regular languages, which a finite automaton recognises with a fixed amount of memory. Keith Duggar has made the matching point about networks on Machine Learning Street Talk: a network of fixed size is a finite-state machine, not a Turing machine. There is also a theorem that says exactly how well transformers fit. Yang, Chiang and Angluin proved that transformers with *hard* attention, where each position attends to exactly one other, and a strict causal mask recognise exactly the languages definable in linear temporal logic. Yang and Chiang compiled a temporal logic with counting into ordinary softmax transformers. So every obligation above can be written down as a transformer. And Delétang and colleagues, who trained 20,910 networks on 15 tasks arranged by the Chomsky hierarchy, found that what lets a network carry such structure to longer inputs is an explicit memory it can update, a stack or a tape.

Put these results next to Fact 2 and the picture is odd. The idealised transformer can express every obligation, but it has nowhere to keep one, so it must express it again at every step, through attention. With hard attention that costs nothing, since one position is found exactly. With the soft attention we actually train, every re-expression is paid for out of the shared budget of Fact 1, and that is where the failures come from.

### Competence and performance

In *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965), Chomsky separated what a speaker knows, *competence*, from what a speaker does, *performance*. Linguistic theory, he wrote, is about an ideal speaker

> unaffected by such grammatically irrelevant conditions as memory limitations, distractions, shifts of attention and interest, and errors (random or characteristic) in applying his knowledge of the language in actual performance.

The results about what transformers can express are statements about competence. The three failures in the next section are performance, and they fall under Chomsky's own headings: memory limitations (no state), distractions (the text nearest at hand), shifts of attention (dilution). The same split shows up in practice. Methods that ask a model to criticise and revise its own draft, such as Self-Refine (Madaan et al. 2023), do improve the draft, which they could not do unless the model could see what was wrong. Checking is a competence the model has. Subbarao Kambhampati, on Machine Learning Street Talk, draws the practical conclusion: let the model propose and let outside checkers judge, in a loop he calls LLM-Modulo. The Ledger moves the checkers inside. They teach the network during training, and a small state carries their verdicts while it writes.

Chomsky himself has been a severe critic of language models. He discussed them on Machine Learning Street Talk in 2022, and in the *New York Times* in 2023 he and his co-authors called ChatGPT "a lumbering statistical engine for pattern matching". I don't need to settle that argument here. I borrow his distinction because it tells me where to look, which is not at what the model knows but at the machinery that has to carry what it knows through a long performance.

## Three ways to lose the thread

Now the shapes can be put to work. An invariant can break at any step. An eventuality can only break at the end, when the text stops with the debt unpaid. A count breaks when the text stops at the wrong number because nothing was counting. That makes three moments, and I will give one mechanism for each, each derived from the two facts. The middle one is where the length exponent comes from.

### Dilution

This is how the double dollars fade. They were set in the example posts, thousands of tokens before the fourth paragraph, and every token written since has joined the crowd they compete with.

**Lemma 1.** Suppose every attention score in a row satisfies $$|s_j| \le B$$. Then every weight satisfies

$$
a_j = \frac{e^{s_j}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} e^{s_i}} \le \frac{e^{B}}{n\, e^{-B}} = \frac{e^{2B}}{n}.
$$

*Proof.* The numerator is at most $$e^B$$, and each of the $$n$$ terms of the denominator is at least $$e^{-B}$$. ∎

The proof is one line, and the consequence is large. The $$m$$ tokens of an instruction receive at most $$m e^{2B}/n$$ of a head's attention, and their contribution to what the head passes on is at most that fraction of the largest value vector. While the model writes, $$n = n_0 + t$$ grows by one with every token. The lemma is only a ceiling. A model can live below it and still work, but it cannot hold a fixed instruction's share unless its scores grow, and we can say exactly how fast they must grow.

**Corollary 1.** Let $$m$$ instruction tokens have a score $$\Delta$$ above the other $$n - m$$ tokens, which share a common score. Their share of the head is at least $$c \in (0, 1)$$ if and only if

$$
\Delta \ \ge\ \log \frac{c}{1 - c} + \log \frac{n - m}{m}.
$$

*Proof.* The share is $$a = m e^{\Delta} / (m e^{\Delta} + n - m)$$. Rearranging $$a \ge c$$ gives $$e^{\Delta} \ge \frac{c}{1-c} \cdot \frac{n - m}{m}$$. ∎

So the edge an instruction needs grows like $$\log n$$. A network whose scores are bounded by $$B$$ has an edge of at most $$2B$$, which means its instruction drops below any share $$c$$ once $$n > m\bigl(1 + e^{2B}(1-c)/c\bigr)$$. That is a fixed length, set by the weights, beyond which the instruction is simply too faint.

I like this corollary because it explains, in a few lines, several things people found separately. Veličković and colleagues proved that softmax attention must disperse as its inputs grow. Nakanishi's scalable softmax multiplies every score by $$s \log n$$, which supplies exactly the logarithmic growth the corollary asks for. Trained models park their spare attention on the first few tokens, the attention sinks of Xiao and colleagues. And Federico Barbero's "Transformers need glasses!", discussed on Machine Learning Street Talk, shows the end of the road, where distinct inputs end up with nearly identical final representations. Rotary positions add a second tax, since Su and colleagues showed that rotary attention decays with relative distance. An instruction at the start of a long context is penalised twice, once for the size of the crowd and once for its distance from it.

Scalable softmax grows the edge like log n, as Corollary 1 requires. The Ledger's read is normalised over its own K slots, so its share does not depend on the length of the context at all."></div>

What would fix it? A channel whose normalisation leaves the context out. A lamp of its own.

### Compounding, and the rate

This is how a promise kept for three paragraphs breaks in the fourth. An invariant has to hold at every opportunity to break it, and there are many opportunities in a long text. Let $$h_i(t)$$ be the chance that invariant $$i$$ breaks for the first time at step $$t$$, given that it has held until then. Engineers call this a *hazard*, the word they use for the failure rate of a machine part. If the invariants fail independently, the chance that all $$k$$ of them survive $$L$$ steps is a product,

$$
S(L) = \prod_{i=1}^{k} \prod_{t=1}^{L} \bigl(1 - h_i(t)\bigr),
$$

and a product of many numbers a little below 1 shrinks exponentially. Everything now depends on what the hazards do as the text grows, and for that I need one assumption. I want to state it openly, because the rest leans on it.

**The hazard model.** The hazard of invariant $$i$$ at step $$t$$ depends on the past only through the largest share of attention that any head gives to the evidence for it, $$a_i(t)$$. So $$h_i(t) = g_i(a_i(t))$$, where $$g_i$$ never increases (more attention never makes a slip more likely), $$g_i$$ is continuous at 0, and $$g_i(0) = h_i^{\infty}$$ is the hazard of an invariant that nobody is looking at, less than 1. Given the past, different invariants fail independently.

This is a model, not a law of nature. Evidence can travel through the network in ways that one attention share does not capture, and failures can be correlated. The experiment below tests three of the model's consequences, and they hold, but I would not want anyone to forget that it is an assumption.

**Proposition 2 (the length exponent).** Write $$\kappa = -\lim_{L \to \infty} \frac1L \log S(L)$$.

(a) *One shared softmax.* If the evidence for invariant $$i$$ is $$m_i$$ tokens of a prompt of $$n_0$$ tokens, and every score is bounded by $$B$$, then for every $$n_0$$

$$
\kappa = -\sum_{i=1}^{k} \log\bigl(1 - h_i^{\infty}\bigr).
$$

(b) *A separate read.* If the evidence for invariant $$i$$ is also held by one of $$K$$ slots, read through a softmax over the slots alone in which slot $$i$$ scores at least $$\Delta_i$$ above every other slot, then for every $$n_0$$ and every $$L$$

$$
S(L) \ \ge\ \prod_{i=1}^{k} \bigl(1 - g_i(c_i)\bigr)^{L}, \qquad c_i = \frac{e^{\Delta_i}}{e^{\Delta_i} + K - 1},
$$

so $$\kappa \le -\sum_i \log\bigl(1 - g_i(c_i)\bigr)$$, a bound that mentions neither the prompt nor the length of the text.

*Proof.* Under the hazard model, the chance of no break at step $$t$$, given the past, is $$\prod_i \bigl(1 - g_i(a_i(t))\bigr)$$. If, at every step, that number lies between two bounds that do not depend on the past, then the chance of surviving $$L$$ steps lies between the products of those bounds. (a) By Lemma 1, every head gives the evidence at most $$b_i(t) = \min\{1,\, m_i e^{2B}/(n_0 + t)\}$$. Since $$g_i$$ never increases, $$g_i(b_i(t)) \le h_i(t) \le h_i^{\infty}$$, which squeezes $$S(L)$$ between $$\prod_t \prod_i \bigl(1 - h_i^{\infty}\bigr)$$ and $$\prod_t \prod_i \bigl(1 - g_i(b_i(t))\bigr)$$. As $$t$$ grows, $$b_i(t) \to 0$$ and so $$g_i(b_i(t)) \to h_i^{\infty}$$, which gives both products the same exponent. (b) A softmax over $$K$$ slots gives slot $$i$$ at least $$c_i$$ of its weight whatever the context holds, so $$h_i(t) \le g_i(c_i)$$ at every step. ∎

What does this say, in words? With the shared softmax, the exponent ends up equal to that of an obligation whose evidence is never read. For a long text the model keeps the promise about as well as it would by guessing. And the length of the prompt drops out of the answer. A longer prompt does not change where $$\kappa$$ ends up, it only makes the model get there sooner, because the evidence starts out fainter. Of everything in this post, I find that the least intuitive. With a separate read, the exponent stops depending on any length. It is not zero, and a slip can still happen, but its chance no longer creeps up as the page fills.

The same arithmetic lies behind the collapse that Dziri and colleagues measured in problems with many steps, where small error rates per step multiply. A long post is a problem with many steps, and every paragraph is a step for every invariant at once:

<div data-survival data-caption="The chance that all k obligations survive. Each one's hazard either stays at its opening value, as if attention to the prompt never faded, or climbs towards p∞ as the prompt recedes, with τ setting how fast. Over a thousand words, small rates add up."></div>

Two predictions follow, and the experiment tests both. The first is about sparse and dense invariants. An invariant used on every line, like the font of each paragraph, can be read back from the model's own recent output. One used rarely, like the delimiter of a formula that turns up once every few hundred words, must be found in the examples each time, across a widening gap. So, counted per opportunity to break, a sparse invariant should be more fragile than a dense one. (A dense invariant can still fail more often per post, simply because it gets more chances.) The second prediction is that the product form should get whole-post failure rates right, starting from nothing but the hazards per opportunity, including the rate at which all the invariants survive together.

The cure for compounding is to carry the obligation as state, so that each step reads it instead of rebuilding it, and to give that state a read the crowd cannot dilute. Better still, where it is possible, remove the choice altogether. The section on symmetry shows when that is possible.

### Blind stopping

This is how the post ends after seven paragraphs, without its sources. Eventualities and counts are judged only at the end, and the end is itself a token, $$\langle\text{eos}\rangle$$.

**Proposition 3.** Suppose that at each step while an eventuality $$q$$ is owed, the model ends the text with probability $$e$$, and otherwise pays $$q$$ with probability $$d$$. Then

$$
P(\text{the text ends with } q \text{ unpaid}) = \sum_{t \ge 0} e\,\bigl((1 - e)(1 - d)\bigr)^{t} = \frac{e}{e + d - e d}.
$$

*Proof.* The text ends unpaid at step $$t$$ if it neither ended nor paid at each earlier step, which has probability $$((1-e)(1-d))^t$$, and then ends. Sum the geometric series. ∎

With rates that change over time the same sum holds term by term. What decides the outcome is the ratio of the rate of stopping to the rate of paying, and nothing in a transformer ties the first to what is owed. The decision to stop is one entry of one softmax, computed from the same diluted state as everything else. Two results that deserve to be better known show how fragile that entry is. Stahlberg and Byrne searched translation models exactly and found that, for more than half of all sentences, the single most probable output was the empty one: the models would rather stop. And Newman, Hewitt, Liang and Manning found that teaching a model to predict the end token at all makes it generalise worse to longer outputs, because its hidden states organise themselves around position.

The repair, then, is to make the rate of stopping depend on the debt, so that $$e$$ falls while $$q$$ is owed.

## The Ledger

The Ledger gives the obligations a state, against compounding and for counting. It reads that state through a softmax of its own, against dilution. And it lets the state hold back the end of the text, against blind stopping. It sits beside an ordinary decoder and changes nothing about it:

<LedgerDiagram />

**The clerk.** Once the prompt has been read, $$K$$ learned queries attend over the prompt's hidden states at a middle layer $$\ell_c$$ and write $$K$$ slot vectors $$o_1, \dots, o_K$$, one line of the book per obligation. The clerk runs once per prompt, and this is how implicit rules get in, since it reads them off the examples. Small linear heads on each slot (which font, whether sources are expected, how many paragraphs) are where training can check what it read.

**The state.** This is the margin the scribe never had (Fact 2). Every written token updates one number per slot. With $$h_t$$ the token's normalised state at layer $$\ell_c$$, the chance that this token settles something for slot $$i$$ is $$e_{t,i} = \sigma\bigl(\langle W_h h_t,\, W_o o_i\rangle/\sqrt{d} + b_i\bigr)$$, and the status $$u_{t,i}$$ accumulates it according to the slot's type:

$$
u_{t,i} = \begin{cases}
0 & \text{invariant: always in force} \\[2pt]
1 - \prod_{s \le t} (1 - e_{s,i}) & \text{eventuality: noisy-OR, monotone} \\[2pt]
\sigma\bigl(4\,(\textstyle\sum_{s \le t} e_{s,i} - \hat n_i + \tfrac12)\bigr) & \text{count, with target } \hat n_i \text{ read by the clerk}
\end{cases}
$$

The middle line reads naturally once you see it as a probability: one minus the chance that *none* of the tokens so far paid the debt. The bottom line is a soft version of "have we reached $$\hat n_i$$ yet?". These are the automata of the monitors, written so that gradients can pass through them. An eventuality or count is *pending* with weight $$\pi_{t,i} = r_i (1 - u_{t,i})$$, where $$r_i$$ is the clerk's estimate that the obligation applies at all. An invariant is never pending; it is simply in force.

**The read.** In every layer above $$\ell_c$$, each written token attends to the $$K$$ slots, whose keys and values are built from $$o_i$$ and from $$(u_{t,i}, \pi_{t,i}, r_i)$$. This softmax, one per head as in ordinary multi-head attention, is normalised over the $$K$$ slots and never over the context. The lamp is its own, and Fact 1 no longer applies to it:

$$
x_t \leftarrow x_t + \tanh(g_\ell)\; W_O \sum_{i=1}^{K} \mathrm{softmax}_i\!\left(\frac{q_t \cdot k_{t,i}}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right) v_{t,i}.
$$

**The gate.** Pending eventualities and counts hold back the end of the text. With $$\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$$,

$$
\text{logit}_t(\langle\text{eos}\rangle) \mathrel{+}= \gamma \sum_{i \,\in\, \text{eventualities, counts}} \log\bigl(1 - \pi_{t,i} + \varepsilon\bigr).
$$

While a debt is fully owed ($$\pi = 1$$), this multiplies the odds of stopping by $$\varepsilon^{\gamma} = 10^{-3\gamma}$$, which is exactly the lever Proposition 3 asks for. The toy learned $$\gamma$$ between 0.30 and 0.42 across runs, so the odds of stopping with a debt outstanding were cut by a factor between 8 and 18.

**Training.** Checking is cheap, so the checkers can teach. Next to the usual next-token loss, the clerk's heads learn from a label for each obligation, and the state learns from the monitors' verdicts on every prefix of the training text: whether the sources have begun yet, and, for a count, whether a paragraph starts here. Every token of training text becomes a small lesson in bookkeeping.

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{LM}} + \lambda_{\text{ext}} \sum_i \mathrm{CE}(\text{clerk}_i, \varphi_i) + \lambda_{\text{ev}} \sum_{t, i} \mathrm{BCE}\bigl(u_{t,i},\, [\![ y_{\le t} \text{ has paid } \varphi_i ]\!]\bigr).
$$

### What the Ledger buys

Five properties follow straight from the definitions, and each one matters somewhere later.

1. **It changes nothing when inserted.** With $$g_\ell = 0$$ and $$\gamma = 0$$, $$\tanh(g_\ell) = 0$$ and the gate adds nothing, so the network computes exactly what it computed before. A trained model can be extended without being disturbed. The zero-initialised tanh gate is Flamingo's trick for grafting new inputs onto a frozen model. In the toy, every placement tested below matched the baseline's loss to 12 decimal places at insertion.
2. **Its share does not dilute.** The read is normalised over $$K$$ slots, so by the argument of Lemma 1 a slot with an edge $$\Delta$$ over the others gets at least $$e^{\Delta}/(e^{\Delta} + K - 1)$$ of the read, whatever $$n$$ and $$t$$ are. This is the hypothesis of Proposition 2, part b, and the logarithmic growth of Corollary 1 is no longer needed.
3. **Paid stays paid.** The status of an eventuality or a count can never decrease (a product of numbers between 0 and 1 can only shrink; a sum of non-negative terms can only grow). Like the $$\top$$ of a monitor, once a debt is settled the gate cannot trap the model.
4. **It trains in parallel.** The noisy-OR is a running sum of $$\log(1 - e_{s,i})$$ and the count a running sum of $$e_{s,i}$$, so training needs no back-propagation through time, even though the state is a recurrence.
5. **It is cheap, and you can read it.** A slot's state is $$o_i$$ plus a linear function of three numbers, so its keys and values are $$W o_i$$, computed once per prompt, plus a correction of rank 3. Per written token and read layer that leaves one query projection and $$K$$ dot products, $$O(d^2 + Kd)$$, with no term in $$n$$, where self-attention costs $$O(d^2 + nd)$$. And the slots can be read out, which is rare in a neural network: what the clerk believes is owed, and what the model believes it has paid.

Why not something simpler? Each simpler repair answers part of the problem:

None of the ingredients is new on its own, and I would rather say so plainly. The idea of keeping state beside the language machinery is older than transformers by half a century. William Woods' augmented transition networks, in 1970, carried registers beside the grammar for exactly this kind of bookkeeping, recording what had been found and what was still expected. Closer to our time, the Recurrent Memory Transformer and Block-Recurrent Transformers carry a learned state from one stretch of a long text to the next. What I have not found anywhere is the combination: a state with one typed line per obligation, filled from the examples, taught by monitors, read through its own softmax, and allowed to hold back the end of the text.

For one large class of invariants, more is possible than a lower hazard. Mathematics saw the reason long ago, under the same word.

### Coherence in mathematics

Mathematicians have used the word *coherence* for a long time, with a precise meaning, and it is worth a detour because it is exactly the idea we need. In a monoidal category you can multiply objects, and there are many ways to put brackets into a product such as $$(a \otimes b) \otimes (c \otimes d)$$. Each rebracketing is a chain of associativity isomorphisms, and with four objects there are already five bracketings and several routes between any two of them. Saunders Mac Lane's coherence theorem (1963) says that any two such routes between the same two bracketings are equal. Every diagram built from them commutes, however large. An equivalent form of the theorem says that every monoidal category is equivalent to a *strict* one, in which those isomorphisms are identities and there is nothing left to choose.

I find the second form the more beautiful of the two, and the more useful here. Coherence, in mathematics, is the guarantee that choices made in different places agree. Strictification makes the agreement automatic, by removing the choices.

Many writing conventions are choices of exactly this kind. The double dollars and sentence case are among them: which font, which delimiter, "colour" or "color", each a choice among interchangeable variants. A text keeps such a convention when it makes the same choice everywhere. The Ledger helps the network remember its choice. Strictification suggests something better, which is to make the choice once, outside the network, and leave nothing to remember.

### A group acting on words

Let a finite group $$G$$ act on the vocabulary $$V$$ by permutations, $$\rho : G \to \mathrm{Sym}(V)$$ with $$\rho(gh) = \rho(g)\,\rho(h)$$, extended to texts one token at a time. In the toy below, $$G = S_6 \times S_4 \times S_3$$. One factor permutes the six fonts, one the four heading styles, one the three delimiters (opening and closing together), and every other token stays put. The orbits $$G \cdot v = \{\rho(g)\,v : g \in G\}$$ are the four style families plus single tokens, and a style is a choice of one member from each family. Attention has a symmetry of the same kind, permutations acting on positions instead of words. Without positional encodings and without the mask it is equivariant to any reordering of the tokens, which is why the 2017 paper had to add positions. The Geometric Deep Learning programme of Bronstein, Bruna, Cohen and Veličković, the subject of a special edition of Machine Learning Street Talk, builds whole architectures from symmetries like these, and Gordon, Lopez-Paz, Baroni and Bouchacourt, and Akyürek and Andreas, used groups acting on words to make models generalise.

Write $$C(x, q)$$ for the set of coherent posts, given example posts $$x$$ and a request $$q$$.

**Proposition 4 (equivariance).** For every $$g \in G$$, $$C(\rho(g)\,x,\ q) = \rho(g)\,C(x, q)$$. Relabel the author's examples, and the coherent posts are relabelled the same way.

*Proof.* Each style monitor checks that every token of its family equals the member the examples use, and applying one permutation to both sides preserves that equality. The other monitors never ask which member of a family appears, and from the request they read only the requested length, which $$G$$ leaves alone. ∎

A network that learns this relation from data has to learn it separately for every style it meets, and it sees the rare styles least. In the toy's baseline (two seeds, 600 posts), authors who use the default font had it kept 96.7% of the time, and authors who use one of the other five, 91.2%. For delimiters the figures were 97.2% and 90.4%. The symmetry is in the task, and the model does not know it.

### Gauge fixing

In physics, a gauge is a choice of reference frame that the laws of nature do not depend on, and fixing the gauge removes the redundancy. The same move works here. Choose a canonical style $$c$$, the first member of each family. Read the author's style $$\hat s$$ from the examples, and let $$\hat g$$ be a group element with $$\hat g \cdot c = \hat s$$. In the toy that is a product of three transpositions, which is its own inverse. Then:

1. move the context into the canonical frame, $$x \mapsto \rho(\hat g)^{-1} x$$;
2. let the transformer, trained the same way in the canonical frame, write with its output restricted to the canonical member of every family, so that its alphabet is the set of orbits $$V/G$$ (96 tokens become 84);
3. map the text back, $$y = \rho(\hat g)\, y_c$$.

<FrameDiagram />

**Proposition 5 (invariants by construction).** Under gauge fixing, the invariant of family $$i$$ holds at every position of the text if the frame read it correctly, and fails at every occurrence of that family otherwise. For every length $$L$$,

$$
P(\text{invariant } i \text{ is broken}) \le P(\hat s_i \ne s_i),
$$

with equality whenever the family occurs at all.

*Proof.* The only member of family $$i$$ the network can emit is $$c_i$$, and step 3 maps it to $$\rho(\hat g)\,c_i = \hat s_i$$ wherever it appears. So every occurrence matches the author if $$\hat s_i = s_i$$, and none does otherwise. ∎

**Proposition 6 (reading the frame).** Suppose the examples contain $$m$$ tokens of family $$i$$, each equal to the author's member independently with probability $$1 - \eta$$, where $$\eta < \tfrac12$$. Then the most frequent member is the author's with probability at least $$1 - e^{-2m(1/2 - \eta)^2}$$.

*Proof.* Let $$X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(m, 1 - \eta)$$ count the author's tokens. If $$X > m/2$$ the author's member is a strict majority. By Hoeffding's inequality, $$P(X \le m/2) = P\bigl(X - m(1 - \eta) \le -m(\tfrac12 - \eta)\bigr) \le e^{-2m(1/2 - \eta)^2}$$. ∎

Put the two together and you get the result this section is for.

**Theorem 1 (a length exponent of zero).** Under gauge fixing, with the frame read by majority count and the assumptions of Proposition 6 for each family $$i$$ (with $$m_i$$ examples and noise $$\eta_i$$), the probability that every group-structured invariant holds throughout a text of any length $$L$$ is at least

$$
1 - \sum_i e^{-2 m_i (1/2 - \eta_i)^2}.
$$

The bound does not depend on $$L$$, so whenever it is above zero, the length exponent of these invariants is $$\kappa = 0$$.

*Proof.* By Proposition 5 the text keeps all these invariants whenever every family's frame is read correctly, whatever $$L$$ is. By Proposition 6 each family is misread with probability at most $$e^{-2 m_i (1/2 - \eta_i)^2}$$. A union bound over the families gives the result. ∎

Now the three regimes can be seen side by side, and they turn out to have the same shape. Each is a one-time reading of the prompt followed by a hazard at every step. The shared softmax does no one-time reading at all, and its hazard drifts up towards $$h^{\infty}$$. The Ledger reads once, through its clerk, and keeps a bounded hazard. Gauge fixing reads once, through the frame, and keeps no hazard at all, because it has moved the whole risk into that single reading, before the first word is written. And that one reading gets *more* reliable with more examples, where the hazards got worse with more text. This is strictification in miniature: many places that had to agree, replaced by one choice that cannot disagree.

### What it saves

Gauge fixing saves work as well. Once the clerk and the frame have read the examples, the written tokens need the examples only for their content. When the examples are there only for their conventions, as in the toy, their keys and values can be dropped as soon as they are read. Each written token then attends to the request, its own text and the $$K$$ slots, and a post of $$L$$ tokens that followed $$n_{\text{ex}}$$ example tokens saves $$L\, n_{\text{ex}}$$ key lookups per head and layer. Gist tokens (Mu et al. 2023) compress prompts in the same way, by masking attention during training; here what survives the compression is typed and checked. The output alphabet shrinks as well, from $$V$$ to $$V/G$$, and the network stops spending capacity on relabelled copies of the same behaviour.

There are limits, and they are real. The group must act on the units the model writes. "Colour" and "color" can be cut into different tokens, and lower-casing a heading cannot be undone, so not every convention is a permutation of tokens. Examples that carry content, such as voice, topic and facts, cannot be dropped wholesale; only their conventions can be factored out. And obligations with no symmetry, like "end with sources" or "write nine paragraphs", stay with the Ledger.

## Where coherence belongs

I have been saying "at the output", "inside", "along the text" as if the place were obvious. It is not a free choice, but it can be worked out, because each part of the repair answers one failure and each failure happens in one place in the network.

Start from the residual stream. Elhage and colleagues describe a transformer as a shared channel that every attention layer and feed-forward layer reads from and adds to, and the logits read its final sum. Anything written into the stream at any depth reaches the output directly. What the depth decides is what the rest of the network can do with it.

**Before, in the context.** Written as tokens in the prompt, obligations compete in the same softmax as everything else (Lemma 1), and with rotary positions they also sit further back with every token. This is the placement Corollary 1 rules out.

**Inside, before or after attention.** Added to the stream just before a layer's attention, the Ledger's read can change what that attention looks for, so that "sources are still owed" becomes "find how the examples wrote their sources". Added just after, it reaches the feed-forward layer and the layers above. Either way, the network can compute with it.

**Inside, as modulation.** The Ledger could scale and shift the normalised stream instead of being read by attention. Peebles and Xie compared exactly these options for conditioning diffusion transformers (tokens in the context, cross-attention, and zero-initialised adaptive layer norm), and the last gave the best images at the lowest cost, tokens in the context the worst.

**After, at the output.** Biasing the logits, gating $$\langle\text{eos}\rangle$$ or restricting the alphabet can veto or choose among tokens, exactly, but it cannot make the network look anything up or plan ahead.

**Across, along the sequence.** Attention keeps no state (Fact 2). The obligations are regular languages, whose natural carrier is a small state updated as the text goes by. The Ledger's status is exactly that, a small recurrence running along the positions, beside the attention and not inside it.

The rule that comes out is to act on an obligation at the lowest place where it can be both checked and acted on, and no lower.

| obligation | where it breaks | where to act |
|---|---|---|
| a choice among interchangeable variants (the double dollars, sentence case) | one output token | after: restrict the alphabet (the frame) |
| an eventuality or a count (sources last, nine paragraphs) | the decision to stop | across: a status; inside: a read of it; after: a gate on stopping |
| an order, or anything that needs content retrieved | what is looked up next | inside, before attention |
| voice, topic, facts | everywhere | inside, by ordinary attention |

</div>

The placement screen in the experiment below disagreed, and it was right: a gate can refuse to stop, but it cannot plan the paragraphs that make a count come out right. A count needs its status read inside the network.

A claim about architecture should be tested where everything else is held still. Frontier models cannot be retrained here, so I built the smallest world that has the problem, and designed it so that each numbered result above has a measurement that could have come out against it.

### The task

An *author* has a house style: one of six paragraph fonts, one of four heading styles, one of three formula delimiters, and two habits, a closing "why" section and a sources section, each present or absent. It is the opening scene, shrunk. The fonts, heading styles and delimiters play the double dollars and sentence case, the two habits play sources last, and the request plays the nine paragraphs. The context shows two or three of the author's posts, then a request, a line in the *default* style (plain serif, single-dollar formulas) that says how many paragraphs to write. Then the model writes. The style is never stated. It has to be read off the posts and held against the request, which sits right next to the point where writing begins. The words come from a fixed random chain over 48 words and mean nothing at all. Chomsky's ideas are colourless on purpose: only form is under test.

Most authors in the training data use the default style (40% of fonts, half of all delimiters), so a model that stops looking at the evidence drifts towards the style of the request. Seven monitors check each post for the implicit rules: font, headings and maths (the three invariants), the why-section and the sources (eventualities, with sources required to come last), their order, and form, meaning no token that belongs to the prompt, such as a stray request marker. An eighth checks the requested length, the one explicit instruction and the one count.

### The models

Six variants, identical except for the parts under test. Each is a 3-layer decoder with 4 heads, width 64 and rotary positions (155,456 parameters), trained from scratch for 1,500 steps on fresh batches of 32 episodes with AdamW (peak learning rate 0.002, cosine decay, gradient clipping at 1), with the loss taken only on the post the model writes. The Ledger's two label losses are weighted 0.3 each.

1. **baseline**: the plain decoder.
2. **baseline + same labels**: the plain decoder with the Ledger's supervision attached to linear probes on its hidden states. If the Ledger wins only because it sees more labels, this variant will win too.
3. **Ledger**: clerk at layer 1, reads after attention in layers 2 and 3, $$K = 8$$ slots (three invariants, two eventualities, one count, two left free and unsupervised), the gate on the eventuality and count slots, and supervision (212,427 parameters).
4. **Ledger, one shared softmax**: the same slots, read inside the ordinary attention softmax, as tokens in the context would be. This isolates Fact 1: same state, same labels, shared budget.
5. **Ledger + frame**: the Ledger with gauge fixing.
6. **Ledger + frame, examples dropped**: as 5, but written tokens may not attend to the example posts. Only the clerk and the frame read them, so their keys and values can be thrown away before writing begins.

Each trained model wrote 300 posts, one for each of the same 300 prompts (two example posts each; 5 requested lengths, 60 authors each), sampled at temperature 1 so that the numbers describe the model itself and not a decoding trick. Because every model sees the same prompts, two models can be compared prompt by prompt, with an exact McNemar test.

All of this ran on four CPU cores, with no accelerator. One run takes about twenty minutes to train and ten to evaluate, and I kept the experiment to what fits in that budget instead of stretching it. The baseline and the Ledger were trained with two seeds each; the labelled baseline, the shared-softmax Ledger and the two Ledgers with a frame with one seed each. The remaining ablations did not fit, and they are listed at the end as work still to do. The question of placement got a cheaper screen: all six placements and the baseline, one seed each, trained for 400 steps instead of 1,500 and scored on 100 prompts. A screen like that ranks candidates. It does not settle anything.

### Results, claim by claim

Across 600 posts from two seeds, the baseline kept all seven implicit rules in 80.5% of them (77.3% and 83.7% for the two seeds). The Ledger kept them in 90.0% (89.0% and 91.0%). On the same prompts and seeds, the Ledger succeeded where the baseline failed 84 times, and the reverse happened 27 times; the exact McNemar test gives $$p = 5.5 \times 10^{-8}$$. With the frame the Ledger reached 95.0%, and with the examples dropped 93.7% (one seed each; $$p = 3 \times 10^{-6}$$ and $$1 \times 10^{-9}$$ against the baseline on the same prompts).

Now the claims, one at a time.

**Dilution is real (Lemma 1).** In the baseline, the share of attention that written tokens pay to the example posts halves over the course of a post, from 0.66 in the first ten tokens to 0.34 at tokens 80 to 99, averaged over layers. The Ledger model's ordinary attention fades in exactly the same way, from 0.69 to 0.34. So the Ledger does not stop dilution. It gives the obligations a channel that dilution cannot reach.

<ToyResults chart="attention" caption="Attention that written tokens pay to the example posts, averaged over heads and layers, against how far into the post they are. Lemma 1, measured." />

**The hazards fall in the order the theory predicts.** Take the delimiter of a formula, the sparsest invariant. Per opportunity to break it, the baseline broke it 3.96% of the time (95% interval 2.82% to 5.54%). The Ledger broke it 1.51% of the time (0.88% to 2.57%), and the two intervals do not overlap. The frame broke it never. The same slots inside the shared softmax landed in between, at 2.26%, with an interval that overlaps both of its neighbours, which is what Corollary 1 leads you to expect from slots that have to compete with the context. Shared softmax, separate read, frame: three regimes, three hazards, in that order.

<ToyResults chart="hazard" caption="The chance of breaking an invariant at each opportunity to break it (a paragraph, a heading, a formula), pooled over seeds, with 95% intervals. The count is first breaks over opportunities." />

**Sparse breaks more easily than dense.** Per opportunity, the baseline broke the maths delimiter, which turns up once every few paragraphs, 3.96% of the time, and the font, which starts every paragraph, 0.75% of the time. The sparse invariant is five times as fragile, as predicted. The font hazard also shows the approach that Proposition 2, part a, describes. It is zero at the first paragraph, 0.51% in paragraphs 2 to 4, 1.09% in paragraphs 5 to 8, and 0.84% beyond, rising and then levelling off.

<ToyResults chart="rising" caption="The chance that a paragraph is the first to break the font, among posts that reached that paragraph with the font intact (posts that end are left out of later bins, not counted as survivors)." />

**The product law holds.** Take the hazards per opportunity alone, and each post's number of opportunities, and predict the share of posts that keep each invariant, and all three together. The predictions for single invariants reproduce the measurements closely, but that is partly by construction, since the hazards were estimated from the same posts. The joint prediction is the real test, because it assumes the three invariants fail independently, which nothing forced to be true. It lands within half a point of the measured joint rate for every model.

<ToyResults chart="productlaw" />

Each cell is predicted / measured, in percent. The Ledger lowers every hazard (maths 3.96% → 1.51%, font 0.75% → 0.41%), and the product law carries those lower hazards through to the whole post: 86.5% → 93.2% for all three invariants together.

**The separate budget is what matters (Fact 1).** Put the same slots, with the same state and the same labels, inside the ordinary softmax, and they do not help: 74.0% against the baseline's 80.5%, a difference the paired test cannot tell apart from zero ($$p = 0.29$$, one seed). The why-section and the sources suffered most, kept in 88.0% and 93.7% of posts. Only the normalisation differs between this variant and the Ledger.

**The state is what counts (Fact 2).** The largest single gain is on the count. 98.5% of the Ledger's posts had the requested number of paragraphs, against 38.5% for the baseline. The labelled baseline, which sees the same paragraph labels through probes on its hidden states, managed 40.3%. Supervision without a state to accumulate it hardly helps at all. The labelled baseline is also 82.7% coherent overall (one seed; $$p = 0.08$$ against the baseline), well short of the Ledger. Coherence still falls as the requested length grows, from 85.8% at four paragraphs to 70.8% at sixteen for the baseline and from 95.0% to 87.5% for the Ledger. The drop is halved, not removed.

<ToyResults chart="bylen" caption="Share of coherent posts against the number of paragraphs requested." />

**Blind stopping (Proposition 3).** In this toy, stopping failures are rare even for the baseline, which kept the why-section in 97.0% of posts and the sources in 97.5%. The Ledger raises these to 98.5% and 99.0%, and the gate's learned strength says why: with $$\gamma$$ between 0.30 and 0.42, the odds of ending with a debt outstanding are cut 8 to 18 times. The ablation that would isolate the gate, the Ledger without it, did not fit the budget. So the effect is small, and it is not separately confirmed at this scale.

**Symmetric rules cannot break (Theorem 1).** Under gauge fixing the frame was read correctly for every prompt, and not one invariant broke: no break in 3,006 font opportunities, 1,069 headings and 451 formulas, nor in 3,007, 1,076 and 422 with the examples dropped. That is Proposition 5 observed, and since the examples here never deviate ($$\eta = 0$$), Theorem 1's bound is 1. What still goes wrong for the frame models is everything the frame does not cover: form, the eventualities, the count.

**Dropping the examples costs almost nothing.** It cuts the keys each written token attends to from 119.6 to 43.2, a reduction of 64%, and the attention work with them, while coherence stays at 93.7%. In a model this small the fixed cost of the projections and feed-forward layers dominates, so the total falls less: the full architecture costs about 199,000 operations per written token, the same as the baseline and 13% less than the Ledger alone. In a model with long contexts, where attention dominates, the saving grows with the length of the examples.

## Putting it in a real model

<div class="cx-talk">
<p class="model"><span class="who">Model</span>"Works in a toy" is what everyone says just before a real model humbles them.</p>
<p class="reader"><span class="who">Reader</span>Then go and be humbled, with your predictions written down first.</p>

The toy leaves open the question that matters most: does any of this help a model that already speaks? I have built that test on SmolLM2-135M-Instruct, a pretrained instruction model with 30 layers and a context of 8,192 tokens, measured on IFEval, the standard benchmark of instructions a program can check. The code is public and tested. The runs are not done yet, so this section gives the design and, before any number exists, what the theory predicts.

### IFEval is already written in the three shapes

Every IFEval instruction type but two is one of the obligations of the toy. "No commas" is a promise like the double dollars, "end with a postscript" like sources last, "at least 300 words" like nine paragraphs.

<div class="cx-text">

| shape | IFEval types | instances |
|---|---|---|
| invariant, $$\mathbf{G}p$$ | no comma, forbidden words, all lowercase, all capitals, response language, JSON, wrapped in quotes | 268 |
| eventuality, $$\mathbf{F}q$$ | postscript, title, constrained answer, two responses, repeat the request, end phrase | 164 |
| count, $$\#p$$ | keywords present, keyword frequency, words, sentences, paragraphs, bullets, highlighted sections, sections, placeholders, capitalised words | 357 |
| not supported | letter frequency (letters live inside tokens), first word of the n-th paragraph | 45 |

</div>

So each type gets one slot of its shape, and nothing has to be matched to anything. The monitors are already written, too. The state's training labels are IFEval's own checkers, run on every prefix of a response. For a count, the label marks the token at which a prefix first gains a counted unit; for an eventuality, the token at which it first pays. On a test set of texts these labels agree with the official checkers at every threshold.

### Why the read must be separate, again

A module added to a model that already works should start by doing nothing and learn from there. The Ledger does. Its read enters through $$\tanh(g)$$ with $$g = 0$$, so at insertion the model is unchanged, and yet the gradient with respect to $$g$$ is the read itself, so training moves it at once. The obvious alternative is to add the same slots as extra keys and values in the model's own attention, which is what memory tokens and prefix tuning do. That cannot work the same way, and I learned why the hard way.

In the first version of the retrofit, the control that puts the slots in the model's own softmax had a share $$\lambda = \max(\phi, 0)$$, starting at $$\phi = 0$$ so that the model began unchanged. It never learned anything. Its share sat at exactly zero from the first step to the last, and I went looking for the bug. There wasn't one. An early gradient step had pushed $$\phi$$ just below zero, where the share is flat at zero and every gradient vanishes, and the slots were closed for good. Once I understood it, the failure turned into a small theorem.

**Proposition 7.** Let a frozen head output $$y = \sum_j a_j v_j$$ with $$a = \mathrm{softmax}(s)$$, and add slot values $$w_i$$ with scores $$\sigma_i$$ to the same softmax, with a share multiplier $$\lambda(\phi) \ge 0$$:

$$
y' = \frac{\sum_j e^{s_j} v_j + \lambda \sum_i e^{\sigma_i} w_i}{\sum_j e^{s_j} + \lambda \sum_i e^{\sigma_i}}.
$$

Then $$y' = y$$ on every input only if $$\lambda = 0$$ (unless every $$w_i$$ equals $$y$$ on every input), and if $$\lambda$$ is differentiable, every derivative of $$y'$$ with respect to the new parameters is zero there.

*Proof.* $$y' - y = \lambda \sum_i e^{\sigma_i}(w_i - y) / (Z + \lambda Z_\sigma)$$, with $$Z$$ and $$Z_\sigma$$ the two sums of exponentials, which vanishes everywhere only if $$\lambda = 0$$ or every $$w_i = y$$. At $$\lambda(\phi_0) = 0$$ the nonnegative function $$\lambda$$ is at its minimum, so its gradient is zero, and so is that of $$y'$$ with respect to $$\phi$$. Every term involving the slots' own parameters carries the factor $$\lambda$$. ∎

In plain words, a share of one budget cannot be negative, so a share that starts at exactly zero sits at the bottom of its range, where the ground is flat in every direction. Put into a shared softmax without changing the model, slots never learn. Put in open, they change the model before they have learned anything. It is Fact 1 once again, this time deciding whether a module can be added at all. In the retrofit, the shared-softmax control therefore starts barely open, with $$\lambda = \phi^2$$ and a share of $$10^{-4}$$, free to open or close from there.

### The test

The model stays frozen, and only the new parameters learn. The clerk reads the output of layer 10, with one learned query per slot; numbers written in the prompt reach it as values, and a pointer picks each count's target. The reads follow self-attention in layers 11 to 30, and the gate acts on the end-of-turn token. That is 3.96 million parameters, 2.9% of the model.

Three controls isolate the claims. LoRA adapters with exactly as many parameters, trained on the same data, ask whether it is the architecture or merely the training. The same slots and state inside the model's own softmax ask whether it is the separate budget. The Ledger without its gate asks about the stop. The training prompts are plain writing tasks with one to three instructions, worded by IFEval's own instruction classes but never taken from its test prompts. The targets are the frozen model's own answers: the first of four samples that passes every checker, or else the best one repaired by exact edits, such as lowercasing it or adding the missing postscript, and kept only if it then passes. The evaluation is the official IFEval on its 541 prompts, and then the same prompts followed by up to 6,000 tokens of unrelated text, which varies the prompt length $$n_0$$ exactly as Proposition 2 needs.

It has to run somewhere, and my own hardware is modest, so I sized it to fit a laptop GPU with 4 GB of memory. It also runs on the free GPUs of Google Colab and Kaggle. A notebook for each installs everything, runs the tests and saves its progress as it goes, so that a session that times out picks up where it stopped, and on Kaggle's two GPUs the work is split between them. The machine I built it on could not download the model at all, so every part was tested on a random model of exactly SmolLM2's shape: 264 tests, including one that checks the Ledger leaves the model's outputs unchanged at insertion and one that checks step-by-step writing gives the same answer as a single pass.

### Predictions, before the numbers

Richard Feynman put the first principle of honest science in two phrases: "you must not fool yourself", and "you are the easiest person to fool". Writing predictions down before the runs is the cheapest protection against that I know. Here they are.

1. The Ledger raises IFEval's strict accuracy over the frozen model. Whether it beats LoRA on short prompts is an open question, since both learn from the same data.
2. Its gains are largest on eventualities and counts, the obligations its state tracks.
3. As the unrelated text grows to 6,000 tokens, the frozen model, LoRA and the shared-softmax slots lose accuracy, and the Ledger loses less. This is the prediction that could sink the argument.
4. The shared-softmax slots never beat the Ledger, and fall further behind as the context grows.
5. Without its gate, the Ledger loses most on obligations that must be paid before the end: postscripts, end phrases, minimum counts.

If the third prediction fails, then either dilution is not what limits a model of 135 million parameters on IFEval, or the retrofit does not carry the state, and the next version of this post will say which. The code, its tests, the notebooks and the commands are in [research/ledger-smollm](https://github.com/ivi-137/ivi-137.github.io/tree/main/research/ledger-smollm/).

IFEval has no example posts to imitate, so gauge fixing will have to wait for an imitation benchmark. The recipe carries over unchanged. ## What is proved, what is measured, what is open

With a small budget, the weight of the argument has to rest on the mathematics, so it is worth being exact about which claims are proved and which are only measured.

<div class="cx-text">

The toy is tiny, its prose is noise, and its style has five dimensions where a real blog has hundreds. Its labels come free from the generator, while a real system has to extract them, and extraction errors are a new way to fail. Its symmetry is exact and declared in advance, while real conventions are only roughly permutations of tokens. And the whole theory stands on the hazard model, which the toy supports but cannot prove. The experiments that should come next, in order:

1. The same toy with more seeds and longer training, and the ablations the budget left out: the Ledger without its gate, the Ledger without monitor labels, and the frame without a Ledger.
2. Running the retrofit above, with every control; then larger models, style monitors extracted from examples, and Multi-IF, IFScale and a blog-imitation set like Wang et al.'s.
3. Hazards measured as a function of prompt length in pretrained models, where Proposition 2 predicts the same plateau for every prompt length, reached sooner for longer ones.
4. Contexts of 32,000 tokens and more, where Lemma 1 bites hardest and dropping the examples saves the most.
5. Symmetries that are learned instead of declared, in the spirit of LexSym, for conventions that are only nearly permutations.

## Why the class is NP

Checking whether a draft keeps a set of obligations takes one pass of a few small automata, in time that grows only in proportion to the length of the draft. Writing a draft that keeps them all while also being worth reading is a search through an enormous space, and nothing makes it easy. A proposed answer is easy to check and a good one is hard to find, and that is the shape of NP, the same shape as running Life backwards in [Gliders are messages](/posts/gliders-are-messages/). The Ledger bets that the easy side can teach the hard one, during training, one token at a time. The frame bets that, for some obligations, the hard side can be made to disappear.
